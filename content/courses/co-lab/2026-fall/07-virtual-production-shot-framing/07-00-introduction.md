## Overview

In the XR Studio, students use screenshots from films and other moving image media as visual references for composing virtual production shots. A shot is one continuous camera view. A cut is the transition from one shot to another. A scene may contain many different shots and cuts.

### 1. Select and Analyze a Video Sequence

1. Select a short sequence from a film, television program, animation, trailer, or video game cutscene.
2. Find a continuous section containing at least 4 shots. Approximately 20 - 45 seconds is a useful starting point.
3. Watch the sequence several times, paying attention to composition and the timing of camera changes.
4. Capture a screenshot of each of the 4 shots. Choose frames that clearly demonstrate their composition.
5. Record the title, creator or director, source link, and approximate timestamps of the selected sequence.

Try to select shots with a variety of compositions, distances, or camera angles. For example, a sequence might move from a wide establishing shot to a medium shot, a close-up, and then a reaction shot.

### 2. Add Rule-of-Thirds Overlays in Photoshop

The rule of thirds divides an image into nine equal rectangles using two vertical and two horizontal lines. These divisions can help you examine how subjects, horizons, and important visual elements are positioned within the frame.

1. Open a screenshot in Photoshop.
2. Use the Crop Tool to remove any interface elements or black borders that are not part of the actual image. Preserve the composition and original aspect ratio.
3. Select **View > New Guide Layout**.
4. Set **Columns: 3** and **Rows: 3**, with gutters and margins set to zero.
5. Create a new layer above the image.
6. Using the Line Tool, draw two vertical and two horizontal lines aligned with the guides. Use a visible stroke color and relatively thin lines.
7. Save a JPG or PNG copy with the four lines visible.
8. Repeat for your remaining screenshots.

### 3. Evaluate Shot Composition

For each screenshot, briefly consider:

- **Shot size:** Is it an extreme wide, wide, medium, close-up, or extreme close-up?
- **Camera angle:** Is the camera above, below, or at the subject's eye level?
- **Subject placement:** Is the subject centered, positioned along a third, or deliberately off-center?
- **Headroom and lead room:** Is there appropriate space above the subject or in the direction they are facing or moving?
- **Depth:** How are the foreground, middle ground, and background arranged?
- **Visual emphasis:** What does the composition encourage the viewer to notice first?

The rule of thirds is a compositional tool, not a requirement. Some effective images intentionally use centered compositions, symmetry, unusual framing, or negative space.

### 4. Analyze Camera Cuts and Editing Rhythm

Watch your selected sequence again, this time paying attention to the timing of the edits.

Record the approximate start and end times of at least four consecutive shots and calculate how long each shot lasts.

For example:

<div class="responsive-grid">

| Shot | Duration  | Framing       | Purpose                        |
| ---- | --------- | ------------- | ------------------------------ |
| 1    | 5 seconds | Wide shot     | Establishes the environment    |
| 2    | 3 seconds | Medium shot   | Introduces the action          |
| 3    | 2 seconds | Close-up      | Emphasizes an important detail |
| 4    | 4 seconds | Reaction shot | Shows the consequence          |

</div>

Use the timing of the actual sequence you selected, not the example values above.

Then briefly consider:

- How frequently does the camera cut?
- Does the editing become faster or slower?
- Why might the director have chosen to change shots at these moments?
- Does the sequence use cuts to reveal information, show reactions, follow movement, or create tension?
- How would the experience change if the shots were twice as long or half as long?

A camera cut is not automatically better because it is fast. The timing of each shot should support what is happening and what the audience needs to see.

### 5. Connect the Analysis to Virtual Production

Reflect briefly on how you might use these ideas in your own virtual production work.

Consider how you would reproduce one or more of these compositions in the XR Studio.

What would you need to control?

- Camera position, distance, and height
- Actor or object placement
- Foreground and background relationships
- Virtual environment composition
- Camera movement and available recording space

Think about which shots would be easy to reproduce and which might require additional planning.
